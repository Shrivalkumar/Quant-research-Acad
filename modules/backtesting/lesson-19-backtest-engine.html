Intuition first

A backtest is an accounting machine around a decision rule

Backtesting asks what a precisely specified strategy would have done under a precisely specified execution and accounting model. Market data becomes features, signals, orders, fills, positions, P&L, equity, and metrics. If any transition is ambiguous, the equity curve is ambiguous.

A vectorized engine computes arrays of targets and returns efficiently; Task 1 largely does this. An event-driven engine iterates through dates, cash, holdings, pending orders, and fills; Task 2 does this. Neither style is inherently more realistic. Realism comes from causal information sets, fill rules, costs, corporate actions, and accounting.

Order of operations matters: sell before buy can release cash; stop sell after buy cannot. Marking at stale last close can preserve a position’s value when today lacks a quote. Cash interest, futures carry, and rolls are separate flows. Metrics belong after equity is correct.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

What is the backtest engine doing that my strategy does not?

Your strategy decides what it wants. The engine decides when and at what modeled price orders fill, maintains cash and holdings, applies costs and carry, marks value, and calculates the equity curve.

Why test with two or three bars first?

A full equity curve can hide a one-row shift error. With labeled toy bars, you can manually predict the only correct position and return for each interval.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Freeze the information set

At each event, expose only data available then. This is the engine’s causal boundary.

02

Turn decisions into pending actions

Strategy outputs are validated, stored, and made eligible at the specified future event.

03

Process fills and accounting

Apply execution prices, costs, cash movements, position changes, carry, and marking in a fixed order.

04

Calculate metrics last

Equity must reconcile before CAGR or Sharpe is meaningful. Debug the earliest incorrect balance rather than adjusting a downstream statistic.

Deeper explanation

Portfolio accounting must conserve value

In a cost-free buy, cash falls by the purchase amount while position value rises by the same amount; total equity is unchanged. A transaction cost breaks that equality by reducing equity. In a sale, the position disappears and cash increases by net proceeds. These identities are powerful debugging checks.

For futures, notional exposure can change without spending notional cash, so accounting uses returns on capital plus explicit flows. This is why Task 1 and Task 2 engines look different even though both end in a daily equity series.

Trade lists are secondary records

The equity curve is the primary portfolio outcome. A trade table reconstructs entries and exits for diagnosis, but choices about open trades, carry, overlapping holdings, and attribution can make trade totals differ from equity change.

Never “fix” equity to match an incomplete trade table. Instead, define what the trade record includes, reconcile known differences, and compute portfolio metrics from portfolio equity.

Worked walkthrough

Two-bar sanity case

Bar 0 opens 100, closes 110; signal becomes long only after seeing 110. Bar 1 opens 120, closes 126. Correctly, the strategy does not earn bar 0’s +10% or the gap from 110 to 120. It enters at 120 and earns 126/120−1 = 5% during bar 1, less cost. If your test reports more, the target was applied too early.

Implementation lens

A minimal timing assertion

bars = pd.DataFrame({'open':[100,120], 'close':[110,126]})
want = np.array([1,1])       # decided after each close
pos = np.zeros(2)
pos[1:] = want[:-1]
assert pos.tolist() == [0,1]
assert (bars.close[1]/bars.open[1]-1) == 0.05

Educational pattern—adapt only after you can explain every line.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Starting with full-history metrics before micro-tests
  • Marking a target as held on the signal bar
  • Letting stop-sale cash fund earlier buys
  • Mixing trade returns with portfolio returns
