Backtesting Mistakes That Destroy Strategies

Recognize look-ahead, survivorship, selection, snooping, corporate-action, timestamp, resampling, fill, and benchmark errors.

Intuition first

Most catastrophic backtest errors look reasonable in code

Look-ahead uses information unavailable at decision time. Survivorship excludes securities that disappeared. Selection bias chooses the sample or strategy after observing outcomes. Data snooping tries many rules and reports the winner. Overfitting adapts noise. These problems can coexist.

Full-sample z-scores leak future mean and volatility. Backward-fill imports a later observation into the past. Resampling a daily close and exposing it before day-end leaks. Corporate-action errors create fake crashes or windfalls. Misaligned timestamps award returns before fills. Ignoring costs turns turnover into imaginary alpha.

The Task 2 list was chosen for October 2026 liquidity. Stocks that were liquid in 2022 but later delisted, merged, or became illiquid are missing. A point-in-time daily liquidity filter is still sensible, but it cannot restore absent failures. This likely flatters long-only strategies, especially momentum and small-cap tilts. The honest response is disclosure, conservative interpretation, and stress analysis—not pretending to quantify an unknowable exact correction from the supplied data.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

What is the simplest definition of look-ahead bias?

Your historical decision knows something that the real decision-maker could not have known at that moment. The future input may be direct, or hidden inside cleaning, scaling, ranking, or resampling.

Why is survivorship bias especially dangerous for momentum?

A current survivor list omits some companies that failed, merged, or became illiquid. Past winners selected from only today’s survivors can look safer and stronger than the choices actually available then.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Reconstruct what was knowable

For every historical decision, list the exact rows, universe, adjustments, and execution prices available at that time.

02

Search for indirect future information

Inspect centered windows, backward fill, full-sample scaling, retrospective universe selection, and higher-timeframe resampling.

03

Correct the principle, not the symptom

Replace an invalid method with a general causal rule. Do not delete the dates or symbols that reveal the problem.

04

Rerun all evidence

Bias correction changes features, trades, and metrics. Old results cannot be retained after fixing the process.

Deeper explanation

Survivorship bias changes both selection and measured outcomes

Missing failed companies do more than remove their later losses. They also alter earlier cross-sectional ranks, sector composition, liquidity cutoffs, and the alternatives available at each rebalance. You cannot correct this by subtracting an estimated percentage from final return.

With the supplied data, the defensible approach is to build causal eligibility among available names, state that the October 2026 survivor selection likely flatters results, and avoid precise claims about what a true historical universe would have produced.

Leakage often hides in preprocessing

A strategy can use only historical prices yet still leak through a scaler fitted on all dates, a universe chosen by later liquidity, a backward-filled fundamental value, or a resampled daily close exposed before the session ends. Review the whole pipeline, not just the final signal formula.

A useful test is historical reconstruction: stop the dataset at a chosen date and recompute everything. Every feature, eligible symbol, and decision for that date should match the full-run value. Repeat this at several hand-picked edge cases in addition to the harness check.

Worked walkthrough

Full-sample normalization leak

Suppose a return’s z-score uses the mean and SD from 2021–2024. A 2022 signal then depends on 2023–2024 volatility. Even if no future price appears directly in the feature formula, the scaling leaks. Replace with a rolling or expanding estimate ending at the decision date. Then re-run; do not assume rankings remain unchanged.

Implementation lens

Wrong and right patterns

# WRONG: future-aware global statistics
z_bad = (x - x.mean()) / x.std()

# CAUSAL: estimates through the current row
mu = x.expanding(min_periods=60).mean()
sd = x.expanding(min_periods=60).std()
z = (x - mu) / sd

Educational pattern—adapt only after you can explain every line.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Claiming the public look-ahead check proves causality
  • Deleting SPLPETRO after seeing its effect without a general policy
  • Comparing a non-dividend stock strategy with a total-return benchmark unknowingly
  • Using current constituents for all historical ranks
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. A daily liquidity filter on surviving symbols removes survivor bias?

  1. Yes
  2. No
  3. Only with 60 days
  4. Only if volume adjusted
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Excluded historical failures are still unavailable.

2. Which leaks most directly?

  1. Trailing mean
  2. Centered rolling mean
  3. shift(1)
  4. Next-open fill
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Centered windows include later observations.

3. Why is a sharp single best parameter cell suspicious?

  1. It proves precision
  2. It may be noise or optimization sensitivity
  3. It lowers costs
  4. It increases sample size
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Robust mechanisms usually tolerate nearby settings better than a knife-edge rule.
