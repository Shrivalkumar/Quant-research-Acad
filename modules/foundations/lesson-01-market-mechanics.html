How Financial Markets Actually Work

Follow one order from an investor’s decision through the exchange, trade, settlement, and final portfolio record.

⏱ 45 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked
Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Explain why an exchange needs both buyers and sellers
  • Distinguish bid, ask, spread, liquidity, and price discovery
  • Choose between market and limit orders in a concrete scenario
  • Describe why a trade changes the last price but not necessarily fundamental value

Intuition first

A market is a coordinated auction, not a prediction machine

Imagine Asha wants 100 shares of a company immediately. Her broker routes a buy order to the exchange. The electronic order book contains standing sell offers: 40 shares at ₹100.00, 80 at ₹100.10, and more above. A market order says “buy the best available prices now,” so Asha receives 40 at ₹100.00 and 60 at ₹100.10. The volume-weighted fill is ₹100.06. The new last-traded price may be ₹100.10 even though no analyst changed a valuation model.

The bid is the highest standing price a buyer offers; the ask is the lowest standing price a seller accepts. Their difference is the spread—an immediate cost of demanding liquidity. Deep books can absorb orders with little movement. Thin books force an aggressive order across several levels, creating market impact. Price discovery is the continuous process by which heterogeneous motives—information, hedging, cash needs, indexing, speculation—meet in this auction.

A broker provides access and handles risk checks; the exchange matches orders under rules; clearing and settlement complete obligations. A limit order controls price but risks no fill. A market order controls immediacy but not the final price. Neither order type guarantees profit: execution is only the mechanism that transforms a decision into a position.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

If I buy a share, where does my money actually go?

In an ordinary exchange trade, your broker transfers money through the clearing system and the seller delivers the shares. You usually buy from another investor—not directly from the company. The company receives money when it issues shares in a primary offering; later exchange trading is the secondary market.

Why are there several prices on the screen?

Different people are willing to buy or sell at different prices and quantities. The highest current buying offer is the bid and the lowest selling offer is the ask. A large market order may consume several offers, so there is no single guaranteed price for every quantity.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

A person forms an intention

The trade starts outside the exchange: someone wants to invest, hedge, raise cash, or react to information. The intention must become an order with a side, quantity, order type, and any price condition.

02

A broker validates and routes

The broker checks the account and sends the order to an exchange. The broker is an intermediary; it does not guarantee that the requested quantity exists at one price.

03

The exchange matches compatible orders

A buy can trade with resting sell offers. Price priority and then time priority usually decide which resting orders are matched first. Consuming several ask levels produces a worse average price.

04

Clearing turns a match into ownership

The trade creates obligations: money moves from buyer side and securities from seller side through clearing and settlement. The buyer then has a position whose value changes with later market prices.

Deeper explanation

Price discovery is a negotiation, not a vote on true value

Suppose one investor believes a company is worth ₹120 per share, another needs cash today, an index fund must buy because the company entered an index, and a market maker is willing to provide immediacy for compensation. Their orders meet in the same book even though their motives differ. The resulting price is the level at which a small amount of buying and selling most recently agreed; it is not a certificate that every participant believes the company is worth exactly that amount.

This explains why price can change without news. If urgent buyers arrive when few sellers are available, they lift successively higher asks. If patient sellers place limits far above the market, those orders may never trade. Liquidity therefore concerns both price and quantity. “The stock trades at ₹100” is incomplete unless you also ask how much can trade near ₹100.

From a research signal to a real economic result

A backtest commonly begins with a clean price series, but the investor experiences more stages: observe information, decide, route an order, wait, receive a fill, pay friction, hold risk, and eventually exit. Each stage can change the result. A strategy with a 0.10% expected edge per trade cannot survive a 0.20% round-trip implementation cost. A signal that disappears within minutes may not survive next-open execution.

For this assignment, the harness deliberately simplifies the order book into fixed cost and explicit fill rules. Your research question is therefore not “would every live order fill exactly this way?” It is “does the idea remain convincing under the common accounting model used to compare candidates, and have I stated the missing live-market risks?”

Worked walkthrough

Calculating an aggressive fill

Inputs. Buy 100 shares. Offers: 40 at ₹100.00 and 80 at ₹100.10. Substitution. Cost = 40×100 + 60×100.10 = ₹10,006. Result. Average fill = ₹10,006/100 = ₹100.06. If the midquote before trading was ₹99.95, immediate implementation shortfall is 11 paise per share, before brokerage and taxes.

The attractive but wrong shortcut is to multiply all 100 shares by the displayed best ask. Only 40 shares were available there.

Implementation lens

Representing a tiny book

asks = [(100.00, 40), (100.10, 80)]
remaining = 100
rupees = 0.0
for price, available in asks:
    fill = min(remaining, available)
    rupees += price * fill
    remaining -= fill
    if remaining == 0:
        break
avg_fill = rupees / 100

Educational pattern—adapt only after you can explain every line.

Assignment connection

Why this matters for my H TO H Partners assignment

The harness does not simulate an order book, but its transaction costs and stop-gap rules stand in for real frictions. You must know what simplification is being made before interpreting a backtest as tradable evidence.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Calling the last price “the value” of the company
  • Assuming a market order fills entirely at the best quote
  • Treating zero brokerage as zero trading cost
  • Confusing high trading volume with guaranteed liquidity at every moment
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. The best ask is ₹250 for 20 shares. You market-buy 100 shares. What is guaranteed?

  1. All 100 fill at ₹250
  2. At most 20 can fill at ₹250 before other ask levels matter
  3. The order cannot fill
  4. The spread becomes zero
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Only displayed quantity at that price is available; the remainder may walk up the book.

2. Why can the last price rise without new fundamental information?

  1. Only earnings move prices
  2. An urgent buyer consumes low ask levels
  3. Exchanges randomly change prices
  4. Settlement adds interest
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Order imbalance and liquidity demand can move the marginal clearing price.

3. A limit buy at ₹99 when the best ask is ₹100 primarily controls…

  1. Whether the company succeeds
  2. Maximum purchase price, not whether a fill occurs
  3. Future return
  4. Settlement time
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. The limit protects price but may remain unexecuted.
