Trading and Investing Fundamentals

Translate long, short, flat, cash, leverage, realized P&L, and exposure into exact rupee arithmetic.

⏱ 45 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked
Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Compute long and short profit and loss
  • Separate realized and unrealized P&L
  • Distinguish capital, cash, equity, notional exposure, and leverage
  • Explain why a 1× short can lose more than a long over an unbounded rise

Intuition first

A position is a signed claim on a future price change

A long position benefits when price rises; a short position benefits when price falls. “Flat” means no directional position, not necessarily zero return—Task 1 cash still earns interest. Entry and exit are events; exposure is the amount at risk between them. Portfolio equity is the current marked value of everything owned plus cash, after liabilities.

For 100 cash-equity shares bought at ₹500, notional exposure is ₹50,000. At ₹530, unrealized P&L is ₹3,000. Sell and it becomes realized, less costs. A short reverses the sign: selling borrowed exposure at ₹500 and buying back at ₹470 earns ₹3,000 before costs. If price rises to ₹530, the short loses ₹3,000.

Leverage compares exposure to equity. ₹150,000 exposure supported by ₹100,000 equity is 1.5× gross leverage. The assignment caps Task 1 actual position at −1, 0, or +1 times capital, and Task 2 forbids borrowing. Real futures use margin, so contract notional can greatly exceed cash posted; the harness intentionally simplifies this by modeling 1× capital.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

What is the difference between cash and portfolio equity?

Cash is the uninvested money balance. Portfolio equity is cash plus the current value of all positions. If ₹50,000 of a ₹100,000 account is invested and rises to ₹53,000, cash may remain ₹50,000 while equity becomes ₹103,000.

Why is a short position riskier than simply staying out?

Staying flat has no directional loss. A short loses when price rises, and a price can theoretically rise without a fixed upper bound. Shorting also involves borrowing or derivatives, operational rules, and sometimes carry costs.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Begin with capital

Capital is the wealth supporting the strategy. Decide how much remains cash and how much becomes market exposure.

02

A fill creates a position

Buying produces positive units; a short or negative derivative position produces negative exposure. Entry price records where that exposure began.

03

Prices revalue the position

Before exit, price changes create unrealized P&L and change total equity. Risk is already real even though the trade has not been closed.

04

Exit realizes the round trip

Selling a long or buying back a short ends exposure. Subtract both entry and exit frictions before judging the trade, then place the result back into portfolio context.

Deeper explanation

Four balances you should be able to reconcile

Starting capital is the wealth before a trade. Cash is the uninvested balance after orders. Position value is current units multiplied by the marking price. Equity is cash plus position value, after liabilities. If these quantities do not reconcile, every later return statistic is unreliable.

Imagine ₹100,000 cash and a ₹40,000 purchase. Immediately after a cost-free purchase, cash is ₹60,000, position value ₹40,000, and equity still ₹100,000. If the position rises 10%, position value becomes ₹44,000 and equity ₹104,000. Selling does not create the ₹4,000 gain; it converts an already-existing unrealized gain into cash. Costs make the accounting slightly less symmetrical because equity falls when a trade occurs.

Exposure explains why two equal returns can mean different research

A strategy earning 12% while invested every day is not economically identical to one earning 12% while invested only half the time. The second may have held cash during risky periods, or it may be dependent on a few concentrated trades. Gross exposure sums absolute long and short risk; net exposure subtracts shorts from longs. A portfolio can be market-neutral in net terms and still have large gross risk.

Task 1 simplifies exposure to −1, 0, or +1. Task 2 has several long positions plus cash. When you compare strategies, always connect return to how much and how often capital was exposed. This is why the harness reports time in market rather than only CAGR.

Worked walkthrough

Trade, portfolio, and cash

Start with ₹100,000. Buy 100 shares at ₹500: ₹50,000 exposure and ₹50,000 cash. At a ₹530 close, position value is ₹53,000, so equity is ₹103,000. The ₹3,000 is unrealized. Sell at ₹528 with 0.2% sale cost: proceeds = 100×528×0.998 = ₹52,694.40. If the buy used the Task 2 cost convention, ₹50,000 cash bought only ₹49,900 worth of shares; both sides matter.

Implementation lens

Signed return arithmetic

price_return = exit_price / entry_price - 1
long_return = +1 * price_return
short_return = -1 * price_return
# subtract costs on every change in exposure

Assignment connection

Why this matters for my H TO H Partners assignment

Task 1 returns positions in {−1,0,+1}; understand them as target exposures, not orders. Task 2 returns buy/sell orders and lets the harness maintain shares, cash, and equity. Confusing those two abstractions is a common architecture error.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Using cash and equity interchangeably
  • Calling unrealized P&L “not real” even though it changes risk
  • Ignoring that a short loss grows as price rises
  • Treating target exposure as guaranteed actual holdings before the fill
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. ₹100k equity supports ₹100k long and ₹100k short simultaneously. Gross leverage is…

  1. 0×
  2. 1×
  3. 2×
  4. Cannot be measured
Reveal explanation

Answer: C. Gross exposure sums absolute notionals: ₹200k/₹100k = 2×; net exposure is zero.

2. A long rises from ₹400 to ₹420. Gross return is…

  1. 5%
  2. 20%
  3. −5%
  4. ₹20%
Reveal explanation

Answer: A. 420/400−1 = 5%.

3. When does unrealized P&L become realized?

  1. At every quote update
  2. When the position is closed, under standard trade accounting
  3. Only at year-end
  4. Never
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Closing crystallizes the trade result, though equity changes before closing.
