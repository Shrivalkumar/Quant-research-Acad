Reading Financial Market Data

Read bars as information sets, not pictures; handle timestamps, gaps, adjustments, missingness, listings, and non-trading days.

⏱ 60 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked

  • Explain bar-start timestamps
  • Distinguish split/bonus adjustment from dividend adjustment
  • Handle missing observations without leaking future data

Intuition first

A bar compresses a path—and destroys the order of events inside it

An OHLC bar records the first, maximum, minimum, and last observed price in an interval. It does not reveal whether the high came before the low. Volume counts traded shares for cash stocks; an index has no direct share volume. Task 1 bars are labeled by start time: 09:15 represents the interval beginning then. A 15:15 bar is the final normal bar.

Daily gaps connect yesterday’s close to today’s open. Intraday returns connect open to close. Mixing them assigns risk to the wrong holder. Market calendars contain weekends, holidays, and exceptional sessions; the Task 1 file has one three-bar Muhurat session, so logic based on row count rather than timestamps is brittle.

Task 2 prices are split- and bonus-adjusted but not dividend-adjusted. A split should not look like a 50% economic loss after adjustment. A cash dividend can still cause a mechanical price drop. The README flags a 49.1% SPLPETRO close drop on 7 Jun 2022 as a likely unadjusted event or demerger. Robust code must not explode, but silently deleting every large loss could remove genuine risk.

In long-format stock data, there are no missing cells, but pivoting to date × symbol produces NaNs before listings and on unavailable dates. A stock with only three observations cannot support a 126-day lookback. Missingness is information about availability, not an invitation to backward-fill from the future.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

Does an OHLC bar show the path price took?

No. It gives the first, highest, lowest, and last prices, but not the sequence between the high and low. Any rule requiring that sequence needs finer data or an explicit conservative assumption.

Why are missing stock rows not just zeros?

A zero price would mean the stock became worthless. A missing value means no valid observation is available—often because the company had not listed yet. Replacing missing history with zero or a future value changes the economic meaning.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Establish the observation unit

Decide whether a row is a minute, 15-minute, or daily bar and whether its label marks the start or end. This fixes when each value is known.

02

Audit structural quality

Check order, duplicates, missing values, session lengths, and listing starts. Do not assume a rectangular file means every symbol has equal history.

03

Interpret adjustments

Ask which corporate actions are reflected. Split adjustment, dividend adjustment, and a Total Return Index solve different problems.

04

Choose a causal missing-data policy

Pre-listing values remain unavailable. Forward fill is justified only for some valuation tasks after a valid observation; backward fill imports the future.

Deeper explanation

What information is lost when data is compressed into bars

Consider a 15-minute bar with open 100, high 110, low 95, and close 108. Price may have fallen to 95 before rallying to 110, or risen first and crashed later. A strategy with both stop-loss and profit-target rules can produce opposite results under those two paths. OHLC alone cannot resolve the ambiguity.

Bar-based research should use rules whose outcome is defined by the available fields or use conservative assumptions when multiple triggers could occur. More detailed data can reduce ambiguity, but it introduces larger files, microstructure noise, and stricter timestamp handling. More rows do not automatically mean more reliable evidence.

Corporate actions and the meaning of a return

A two-for-one split roughly halves price while doubling shares; the investor is not economically halved. Split-adjusted history rewrites earlier prices to create continuity. A dividend transfers value from company to shareholder and usually causes an ex-dividend price adjustment; a price-only series may show the price drop without adding the cash received.

Task 2 says prices are adjusted for splits and bonuses but not dividends and warns of possible demerger anomalies. You therefore cannot blindly interpret every extreme close-to-close change as either genuine market loss or bad data. Define a general detection and treatment policy, show sensitivity, and disclose the limitation.

Worked walkthrough

Overnight versus intraday

Yesterday close = 100, today open = 95, today close = 98. Overnight return = 95/100−1 = −5%. Intraday return = 98/95−1 = +3.158%. Total close-to-close = 98/100−1 = −2%. Adding −5% + 3.158% gives −1.842%, which differs because simple returns compound: 0.95×1.03158−1 = −2%.

Task 1’s look-ahead check will not detect every data-cleaning mistake. Task 2’s survivor-selected symbols and varying listing starts make eligibility a research decision. State how anomalies are handled and run sensitivity rather than concealing them.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Assuming high occurred after low
  • Treating bar labels as end times
  • Backward-filling pre-listing NaNs
  • Assuming adjusted means total-return adjusted
  • Dropping every extreme return as “bad data”
