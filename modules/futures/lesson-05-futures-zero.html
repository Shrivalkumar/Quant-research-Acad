Intuition first

A future is a standardized promise whose value moves with an underlying

A futures contract commits economic exposure to an underlying at a future expiry under standardized exchange terms. A Nifty future references an index and uses a contract multiplier or lot size; you do not buy the 50 stocks one by one. A portfolio manager may sell futures to hedge a market decline. A speculator may buy because they expect the index to rise. The same contract transfers risk; motive differs.

Notional is futures price × lot size × contracts. It describes market exposure, not the cash paid. Margin is collateral posted to support that exposure. Daily mark-to-market moves gains and losses through the margin account. Because notional can exceed posted cash, real futures create leverage and margin-call risk.

The assignment removes those operational complications. Position +1 or −1 means exposure equal to current capital, not a chosen number of exchange lots. Cash earns 6% because the model treats capital as parked in a liquid fund while supporting the future. This abstraction makes strategies comparable, but it is not a tutorial for live margin management.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

Do I pay the full futures notional when I trade?

In a real exchange account you normally post margin, which is collateral, while profit and loss respond to the much larger notional exposure. That is why futures create leverage. The assignment deliberately avoids lot and margin complexity and models exposure as at most one times capital.

Why would anyone use futures instead of buying all 50 stocks?

One index future can provide broad exposure with one contract, making hedging and directional trading operationally simpler. It also introduces expiry, margin, basis, and rolling risks that a cash basket does not have.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Read the contract specification

Identify underlying, expiry, lot size, settlement, and margin rules. These define what one contract economically represents.

02

Convert index points into notional

Multiply futures level by lot size and contract count. Profit and loss follow this notional, not merely the cash posted as margin.

03

Mark gains and losses

A long earns when the futures price rises and a short earns when it falls. Daily settlement can add or remove margin cash before the position is closed.

04

Separate real mechanics from the assignment

Task 1 normalizes exposure to capital and omits lot/margin choice. Its purpose is consistent research accounting, not a full exchange simulator.

Deeper explanation

Why futures create leverage even when the price move is small

If a contract has ₹10 lakh notional and requires ₹1.5 lakh margin, a 2% underlying move creates about ₹20,000 gross P&L—13.3% of the margin cash. Leverage has not made the index more volatile; it has made the position large relative to collateral. A sufficiently adverse move can require additional margin even when the long-term view is unchanged.

Real contracts also use lot sizes, expiry dates, settlement rules, and daily mark-to-market. An investor cannot select an arbitrary ₹73,421 notional if the contract multiplier allows only larger increments. Task 1 removes these constraints so every candidate expresses a normalized one-times-capital exposure. That makes research scores comparable but understates operational risk.

Hedging and speculation can use the same order

A portfolio manager owning ₹1 crore of equities might short index futures to reduce broad market risk. A trader with no stock portfolio might short the same futures because they expect a decline. The exchange sees two short contracts; the economic interpretation depends on the rest of each participant’s portfolio.

This matters when discussing “risk.” A losing short future may be a successful hedge if the cash portfolio lost more and the short offset part of the damage. The assignment evaluates Task 1 as a standalone timing strategy, so its short return is judged directly rather than as an offset to another portfolio.

Worked walkthrough

One contract’s P&L

Suppose a future moves from 20,000 to 20,150 and the illustrative lot size is 50. Long P&L = (20,150−20,000)×50 = ₹7,500. Short P&L = −₹7,500. Notional at entry = 20,000×50 = ₹1,000,000; the 0.75% price move creates 0.75% of notional P&L. If only ₹150,000 margin was posted, the cash-on-margin swing is 5%, showing leverage—not a change in economic notional.

Implementation lens

Generic futures arithmetic

notional = futures_price * lot_size * contracts
pnl_long = (exit_price - entry_price) * lot_size * contracts
pnl_short = -pnl_long

Educational pattern—adapt only after you can explain every line.

Assignment connection

Why this matters for my H TO H Partners assignment

Task 1 uses spot percentage returns as the price-change component, then adds cash and subtracts signed carry, costs, and rolls. You are not asked to simulate NSE margin or choose lot counts. In the interview, state that boundary clearly.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Calling margin the price of the future
  • Computing P&L on margin rather than notional
  • Forgetting a short future profits from a decline
  • Assuming the assignment reproduces exchange settlement in full
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. Futures notional is ₹10 lakh and margin posted is ₹1.5 lakh. A 1% underlying fall gives a long approximately…

  1. ₹1,500 gain
  2. ₹10,000 loss
  3. ₹15,000 loss
  4. No change
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. P&L follows notional: 1% of ₹10 lakh = ₹10,000 loss.

2. Why might an investor short an index future without being bearish?

  1. To hedge a long stock portfolio
  2. Futures cannot be hedges
  3. To receive dividends
  4. To remove all basis risk
Reveal explanation

Answer: A. The short may offset existing market beta.

3. In Task 1, +1 means…

  1. One exchange contract
  2. 1× capital modeled long exposure
  3. Unlimited leverage
  4. All cash spent on index shares
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. The harness normalizes notional to capital.
