Returns, Compounding, and P&L

Calculate simple and log returns, compounded wealth, portfolio P&L, fees, and the difference between percentage points and percent change.

Intuition first

Returns multiply through time even when percentages look additive

A simple return is ending value divided by starting value minus one. It answers the investor’s percentage gain for that interval. Wealth compounds as a product of one plus each return. A +10% gain followed by a −10% loss does not return to the start: 1.10×0.90 = 0.99, a 1% loss.

Log return is the natural log of the price ratio. Log returns add across time, which is convenient for time-series analysis, but cash wealth still follows compounded simple returns. Cross-sectional portfolio return is a weighted sum of contemporaneous simple asset returns when weights are measured at the start of the interval.

Gross performance ignores costs; net performance includes them. A 0.2% cost each side is not “only 0.4% once” if the portfolio turns over repeatedly. Costs reduce capital that would otherwise compound, and small frictions can dominate weak, high-frequency edges.

A percentage-point difference is subtraction of rates: Sharpe is not a percent, but 20% versus 15% return differs by five percentage points and 33.3% relative to 15%.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

Why does +10% followed by −10% lose money?

The loss applies to a larger base. ₹100 grows to ₹110; losing 10% of ₹110 removes ₹11, leaving ₹99. Percentage returns compound multiplicatively.

When should I use log returns?

Log returns are convenient for adding changes through time and for some statistical models. Portfolio wealth, fees, and investor gains are most naturally interpreted with simple returns. Use the return definition that matches the question.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Compute one-period change

Divide ending value by starting value and subtract one. Keep the decimal form—0.05 means 5%—during calculation.

02

Apply the change to wealth

Multiply wealth by one plus the return. Costs reduce the amount entering this multiplication.

03

Chain many periods

Multiply every growth factor. Arithmetic averages do not generally equal compound growth.

04

Separate attribution from outcome

Portfolio return combines position returns, weights, cash, and costs. A gross signal return is not the same as the investor’s final net return.

Deeper explanation

Arithmetic mean, geometric mean, and CAGR answer different questions

The arithmetic mean is the average one-period return. The geometric mean is the constant one-period growth rate that reproduces compounded wealth. Volatility makes the geometric result lower: +20% and −20% average to 0% arithmetically but compound to −4%, equivalent to about −2.02% per period geometrically.

CAGR applies the same endpoint idea across years. It is useful for comparison but hides the route between endpoints. A strategy can report a strong CAGR after recovering from a devastating drawdown, so pair it with path metrics and the underlying yearly returns.

Why turnover makes small costs compound into a large problem

Suppose a portfolio turns over its full value six times per year and pays roughly 0.2% on each bought or sold notional. A rough annual friction can become multiple percentage points before market impact, depending on the turnover definition. The exact drag must be obtained from the engine because sales, buys, changing equity, and compounding interact.

Costs also change strategy selection. A fast rule may have higher gross prediction but lower net return than a slower rule. Do not optimize indicators first and “add costs later”; friction is part of the economic experiment from the beginning.

Worked walkthrough

A fee-aware round trip

Allocate ₹100,000 under Task 2. Buy cost leaves ₹99,800 invested. Price rises 5%, so gross position value becomes ₹104,790. Sell proceeds after 0.2% cost = ₹104,790×0.998 = ₹104,580.42. Net return = 4.5804%, not 5%−0.4%=4.6% exactly, because the second fee applies to the larger sale value.

CAGR example: ₹100 grows to ₹144 in two years. CAGR = 144/1001/2−1 = 20%, even if individual calendar-year returns differed.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Adding simple returns across time
  • Subtracting fee rates from CAGR without re-running equity
  • Using average return as compound growth
  • Confusing a five-point increase with a 5% relative increase
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. +20% then −20% produces…

  1. 0%
  2. −4%
  3. +4%
  4. −20%
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. 1.2×0.8−1 = −4%.

2. Why do costs hurt compounding?

  1. They only change trade count
  2. They remove capital that cannot earn later returns
  3. They increase volatility only
  4. They are refunded annually
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Every cost lowers the future wealth base.

3. 15% to 20% is…

  1. 5 percentage points and 33.3% relative increase
  2. 5% relative increase only
  3. 35 points
  4. The same number
Reveal explanation

Answer: A. Both descriptions are valid but answer different questions.
