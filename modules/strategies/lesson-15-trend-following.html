Trend Following and Market Timing

Compare time-series momentum, breakouts, moving-average states, volatility awareness, and the unavoidable cost of whipsaws.

⏱ 85 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked
Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Explain trend-following’s economic bet
  • Compare long-only, long-short, and flat states
  • Measure the turnover/exposure trade-off
  • Predict failure in choppy and gap-driven markets

Intuition first

Trend followers pay many small reversals for a chance to hold large persistent moves

Trend following takes positive exposure after evidence of an upward move and negative or flat exposure after a downward move. Time-series momentum compares an asset with its own past. A breakout compares price with prior extremes. A moving-average state compares smoothed short and long histories. These are related families, not magical indicators.

The economic story may involve gradual information diffusion, slow institutional flows, or risk premia for holding positions that reverse painfully. The failure mode is clear: in range-bound markets, signals flip after prices have already reversed, creating whipsaws and costs. Long-short designs can profit in sustained declines but lose during violent rebounds; long-only avoids short carry and some crash risk but retains market beta; flat states lower exposure and may earn Task 1 cash.

Volatility normalization can make signals more comparable or reduce exposure in stressed periods, but Task 1 permits only discrete −1/0/+1 positions. A volatility filter therefore changes when you trade rather than fractional size. Every extra filter is another hypothesis and parameter, not free risk reduction.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

Why can trend following lose even when the market later trends?

The signal may enter late, exit after giving back profit, or be whipsawed before the sustained move begins. A trend visible on a finished chart was not necessarily identifiable in real time.

What does staying flat accomplish?

It avoids directional exposure during uncertain states. In Task 1 it also earns modeled cash interest. Flat is therefore an active portfolio state, not missing data.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Choose a horizon

Fast signals react sooner but see more noise; slow signals trade less but lag. Horizon is part of the economic claim.

02

Define states and transitions

Specify when to become long, flat, or short and how to leave each state. Ambiguous state changes create unintended churn.

03

Pay for reversals

Every target change incurs friction and may arrive after price has already reversed. Inspect sequences of losing flips, not only average trade.

04

Test persistence

Delay fills and vary neighboring horizons. A trend signal that survives only one exact setting or immediate fill is weak evidence.

Deeper explanation

Why trend systems have a distinctive payoff shape

Trend rules often accept repeated small losses in sideways markets while seeking participation in occasional large directional moves. This can produce a modest win rate and positively skewed trade distribution. A short backtest with one exceptional trend may therefore look stronger than the underlying evidence.

Inspect how much profit comes from the largest trades and whether removing the best year destroys results. A genuine trend hypothesis can still be concentrated, but the report should explain that dependency rather than hiding it behind an average Sharpe.

Fast versus slow is an economic trade-off

A fast rule recognizes new direction sooner and exits reversals sooner, but reacts to more noise and pays more costs. A slow rule ignores small fluctuations but enters later and gives back more before exit. There is no universally correct speed.

Use sensitivity to look for a region where this trade-off remains acceptable. If one precise lookback captures a particular historical turn while nearby values fail, the apparent timing skill is probably fragile.

Worked walkthrough

Whipsaw arithmetic

A long enters at 100 after an upside signal and exits at 97 after reversal: −3%. It flips short at 97 and covers at 100 after another reversal: short return = 1−100/97 = −3.09%. Before costs the two whipsaws lose about 6.0% compounded. A later trend from 100 to 115 may recover the sequence, illustrating the asymmetric payoff pattern.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Judging trend by one best lookback
  • Adding a regime filter solely because it improves 2020
  • Ignoring cash as the flat-state benchmark
  • Calling lower trade count automatically more robust
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. Trend following typically suffers most in…

  1. Persistent trends
  2. Choppy reversals
  3. Flat cash return
  4. Data loading
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Repeated direction changes create late flips and costs.

2. A slower signal generally trades off…

  1. Less lag for more turnover
  2. More lag for less turnover
  3. No costs for leverage
  4. Higher accuracy guaranteed
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Smoothing reduces churn but reacts later.

3. Time-series momentum ranks…

  1. Stocks against each other only
  2. An asset against its own past
  3. Brokers
  4. Transaction fees
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Cross-sectional momentum is the relative ranking concept.
