Position Sizing and Risk Management

Size positions using capital, volatility, caps, and portfolio interactions while understanding why a stop cannot guarantee portfolio safety.

⏱ 80 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked
Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Calculate fixed-fraction and volatility-targeted sizes
  • Explain correlation risk and risk budgets
  • Distinguish stop loss, trailing stop, and drawdown control
  • Recognize estimation and turnover risks

Intuition first

Risk is produced by position size multiplied by uncertain movement

Position sizing maps conviction and risk estimates to capital. Fixed fractional sizing allocates a stable share of equity. Volatility targeting scales down when estimated variability rises. A risk budget allocates expected risk, not merely rupees. Correlated positions can create one hidden bet even when individual weights look diversified.

A stop loss is an execution rule, not a guarantee. Gaps can fill beyond the trigger, many stops can fire together, and repeated small stops can increase turnover. A portfolio drawdown overlay reduces exposure after losses, but it may sell after damage and miss rebounds. Risk controls trade one failure mode for another.

Task 2 caps each new position at 15% and total positions at 15; those are maximums, not a sizing prescription. Task 1 is discrete 1× or flat/short, so “sizing” is mainly state exposure. Reducing time in market can improve Sharpe if avoided risk is poorly compensated, but it can also turn performance into a few concentrated bets.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

Why is equal weighting not equal risk?

A volatile stock can contribute much more uncertainty than a stable stock at the same rupee weight. Correlated holdings can also move together, turning several positions into one large common bet.

Why can a risk control hurt performance?

Reducing exposure after volatility or losses may happen after prices have already moved and can miss rebounds. Risk management changes the distribution of outcomes; it does not create a free improvement.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Measure available equity

Position sizes should respond to current portfolio value, not stale starting capital, unless fixed-notional behavior is intentional.

02

Estimate risk cautiously

Historical volatility and correlation are noisy. Use minimum observations, caps, and sensible behavior when estimates approach zero.

03

Apply portfolio constraints

Per-name caps, total cash, number of holdings, and common-factor concentration operate together. Passing each name cap is not sufficient.

04

Observe the feedback loop

Losses reduce equity and can reduce future size; volatility spikes can force deleveraging. This path dependence affects turnover and recovery.

Deeper explanation

Portfolio risk is not the sum of individual risk labels

Two 10%-volatility stocks do not automatically create a 10%-volatility two-stock portfolio. Their covariance matters. If they move together, diversification is small; if movements are partly independent, combined variability can fall.

Sector labels are a useful first diagnostic but not a complete risk model. Different companies can share market beta, financing sensitivity, commodity exposure, or ownership flows. Examine correlation and stress-period behavior in addition to count and nominal weights.

Drawdown control can become procyclical

A rule that reduces exposure after losses sells when measured risk is already high. If the decline continues, it protects capital; if prices rebound sharply, it locks in lower participation. This is procyclical behavior—risk falls after damage and rises after recovery.

Test the overlay separately from the signal. Measure whether lower drawdown comes from genuine timing skill, simply lower average exposure, or fortunate historical transitions. Compare against a constant lower-exposure baseline.

Worked walkthrough

Volatility-aware weight

Target single-name contribution is 1% daily volatility and estimated stock volatility is 4%. Raw weight = 1%/4% = 25%. A 15% cap reduces it to 15%. If estimation falls to 2%, raw weight jumps to 50%, still capped. This shows why caps protect against unstable low-volatility estimates.

Implementation lens

Capped sizing

raw_weight = risk_target / estimated_vol
weight = min(raw_weight, 0.15)
weight = max(weight, 0.0)
# portfolio cash and total exposure constraints still apply

Educational pattern—adapt only after you can explain every line.

Assignment connection

Why this matters for my H TO H Partners assignment

The Task 2 harness sizes a new buy from portfolio value at the decision close, then caps by cash at execution. The fill can gap, so shares are computed at the actual next price. No adding to an open position is allowed.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Sizing from entry price instead of current equity
  • Assuming 15 positions means diversified
  • Using a near-zero volatility estimate without a floor/cap
  • Treating a stop as a guaranteed maximum loss
Retrieval practice

  2. No, it is an upper bound
  3. Only monthly
  4. Only after profit
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Portfolio design may choose lower weights and cash.

2. Ten equal-weight sector peers primarily reduce…

  2. Name-specific risk more than shared sector risk
  3. Gap risk completely
  4. Costs

Answer: B. Common-factor exposure remains.

  2. Overnight gaps and fill rules
