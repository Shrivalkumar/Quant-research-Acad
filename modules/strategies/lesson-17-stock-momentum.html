Stock Momentum and Relative Strength

Separate absolute, cross-sectional, and time-series momentum; understand ranking, crashes, concentration, and empirical evidence.

⏱ 90 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked

Intuition first

Momentum is an empirical tendency, not a law of motion

Absolute momentum asks whether a stock’s own return over a lookback is positive. Cross-sectional momentum ranks stocks against contemporaries and selects relative winners. Time-series momentum is the broader own-history directional concept often studied across asset classes. A stock can rank first with a −5% return if every other stock fell more; relative strength is not necessarily positive trend.

Jegadeesh and Titman documented intermediate-horizon winner-minus-loser behavior in their historical sample. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen documented time-series momentum across liquid futures in their sample. These are empirical findings with specific portfolios, periods, and frictions—not guarantees that a six-month top-10 NSE strategy will work.

Momentum may reflect underreaction, herding, or compensation for crash risk. It can reverse violently after market rebounds, concentrate in sectors, and become expensive to trade. Volatility adjustment changes the hypothesis from “largest return” to “return relative to variability.” Skipping a recent month, adding an absolute filter, or using a market regime are distinct research choices that consume search degrees of freedom.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

What is the difference between absolute and relative momentum?

Absolute momentum asks whether a stock itself rose or fell. Relative momentum asks whether it did better than peers. The best relative performer can still have a negative return.

Why might momentum suddenly crash?

Prior losers can rebound sharply when market conditions turn, while crowded winners underperform. A strategy exposed to that pattern may lose rapidly even after a long profitable period.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Define the comparison

Absolute momentum compares with zero or another fixed hurdle; cross-sectional momentum compares stocks on the same date.

02

Build historical eligibility

Require enough observations and point-in-time liquidity before computing or ranking. The future survivor list remains a limitation.

03

Rank and examine concentration

Identify whether selected winners cluster in sectors, volatility, or market beta. A rank is not a diversification rule.

04

Challenge persistence

Study reversals, costs, holding horizon, market regimes, and top-trade concentration. Literature motivates the hypothesis but does not validate this dataset.

Deeper explanation

A ranking is a relative statement with hidden bets

The top ten scores may all belong to one sector that recently rallied. The portfolio then expresses sector momentum more than independent stock momentum. Volatility-adjusting or imposing sector limits changes this exposure, but also changes the hypothesis.

Diagnose each rebalance snapshot: score distribution, sectors, market capitalization, liquidity, volatility, and common market beta. A robust narrative should identify what the rank actually selected, not merely call every chosen stock a winner.

Why the six-month warning is scientifically useful

The template demonstrates the interface with a familiar six-month top-ten rule, and the assignment says this naive approach fails hidden data. That warns against treating one strong public-period result as sufficient. It does not reveal which alternative parameter will pass.

Use the warning to investigate structural fragility: concentration, high beta, crash exposure, turnover, survivor bias, and dependence on a market regime. Predeclare changes based on those mechanisms, not on speculation about July 2024–October 2026.

Worked walkthrough

Volatility-adjusted ranking

Stock X returns 20% with 40% annualized volatility; score = 0.50. Stock Y returns 14% with 14% volatility; score = 1.00. Raw momentum ranks X first; risk-adjusted momentum ranks Y first. Neither is universally correct—the scores embody different hypotheses and may produce different sector exposures.

Implementation lens

Point-in-time score

mom = close / close.shift(126) - 1
vol = close.pct_change(fill_method=None).rolling(63).std()
eligible = close.notna().rolling(126).sum() == 126
score = (mom / vol.replace(0, np.nan)).where(eligible)
rank = score.rank(axis=1, ascending=False, method='first')

Educational pattern—adapt only after you can explain every line.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Assuming top-ranked means positive return
  • Using today’s survivor list as point-in-time history
  • Treating a famous paper as local validation
  • Adding many momentum variants and reporting one
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. A stock can rank first with negative momentum when…

  1. Impossible
  2. All stocks are negative and it fell least
  3. Volume is zero
  4. It is Nifty
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Cross-sectional rank is relative.

2. Volatility-adjusted momentum changes…

  1. Only formatting
  2. The economic score and likely portfolio
  3. The exchange
  4. The number of dates
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. It favors return per unit of estimated variability.

3. Academic momentum evidence implies…

  1. Guaranteed NSE alpha
  2. A hypothesis worth testing with local costs and bias controls
  3. No crash risk
  4. Six months is always optimal
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Empirical findings need contextual replication.
