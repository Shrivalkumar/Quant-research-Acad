Understanding Financial Time Series

Reason about lags, autocorrelation, nonstationarity, volatility clustering, regimes, and rolling versus expanding windows.

⏱ 70 min● Concept + calculation + practice⌁ H TO H assignment linked
Learning objectives

By the end, you can…

  • Explain why time order forbids random train/test splits
  • Interpret lag and autocorrelation
  • Distinguish trend from stationarity
  • Choose rolling versus expanding estimates based on a hypothesis

Intuition first

Time is part of the data-generating process

A time series is ordered. A lagged variable uses an earlier observation; reversing that relation creates look-ahead. Autocorrelation measures linear association between a series and its own lags. Price levels often trend and are nonstationary; returns may be closer to stationary, yet their volatility changes.

Volatility clustering means large moves tend to be followed by large moves in either direction. Regimes are persistent environments—trending, choppy, calm, stressed—but their boundaries are known clearly only in hindsight. A structural break changes relationships: participants, regulations, market microstructure, or macro conditions may differ.

A rolling window forgets old data and adapts faster; an expanding window uses all history and is more stable. Neither is universally correct. Both must end at the current decision time. A centered rolling window is invalid for trading research because it includes the future.

Questions beginners actually ask

Clear answers before we add mathematics

Why can I not randomly shuffle market dates for training and testing?

Shuffling lets later regimes influence model choices intended for earlier dates and destroys serial dependence. A real researcher always moves from past information toward a later evaluation period.

What is a market regime?

It is a period with persistent characteristics—perhaps high volatility, strong trend, or rapid reversal. Regimes are useful descriptions, but their start and end are usually obvious only after the fact, so a live regime rule must use observable data.

Follow the mechanism slowly

What happens first, second, third, and fourth

01

Preserve time order

Features at t use t and earlier; evaluation occurs later. Never let later regimes enter an earlier decision through splitting or normalization.

02

Choose memory

A rolling window adapts and forgets; an expanding window stabilizes and remembers. The right choice depends on how quickly the process may change.

03

Inspect dependence

Returns, absolute returns, and features can be autocorrelated. Overlapping lookbacks share observations and therefore are not independent experiments.

04

Expect change

Structural breaks alter relationships. Validation should span different conditions and conclusions should remain conditional on the observed history.

Deeper explanation

Stationarity is an assumption with degrees, not a magic label

A strictly stationary process keeps its whole probability distribution unchanged through time; weaker definitions concern mean, variance, or dependence. Financial price levels usually violate simple stationarity because they wander. Returns may have a more stable mean but still show changing volatility and tail behavior.

Many statistical methods work best when relationships are reasonably stable. Before applying them, ask what part of the process is assumed stable and over what horizon. Rolling estimation is one practical response to change, but choosing a short window increases estimation noise.

Regime filters can create hindsight stories

After a crash, it is easy to label the preceding month “high-risk.” A valid regime feature must have identified the state using data available then. If a filter is invented because one historical year hurt performance, the improvement may be selection rather than foresight.

Test a regime hypothesis by predicting more than return: expected exposure, turnover, false exits, and behavior around transitions. Use several episodes and nearby thresholds. A filter that removes one drawdown while damaging all other periods may not represent a stable mechanism.

Worked walkthrough

Rolling volatility

Suppose five daily returns are 1%, −1%, 4%, −3%, 2%. A three-day rolling SD at day 3 uses days 1–3; at day 5 it uses days 3–5. The day-5 estimate may respond to the recent 4% and −3% moves, while an expanding estimate also retains early calm data. The research question—not convenience—should choose memory length.

Failure prevention

Common mistakes

  • Randomly shuffling time-series rows before validation
  • Calling a regime using its eventual end date
  • Using centered windows
  • Assuming an expanding estimate is automatically unbiased
Retrieval practice

Exercises

1. Why is random cross-validation risky for market time series?

  1. It is slow
  2. It can mix future regimes into training
  3. It uses too little data
  4. It cannot calculate mean
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. Chronology and dependence are broken.

2. Volatility clustering means…

  1. Returns keep the same sign
  2. Large absolute moves tend to cluster
  3. Prices are stationary
  4. Volume is constant
Reveal explanation

Answer: B. It concerns magnitude persistence, not direction.

3. A rolling window compared with expanding typically…

  1. Forgets older data
  2. Uses future data
  3. Has no parameters
  4. Guarantees stationarity
Reveal explanation

Answer: A. Rolling memory is finite.
